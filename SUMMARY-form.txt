TRACK: Transparency & Accountability

Live page: https://eltonlaice.github.io/quinhao/
Repository: https://github.com/eltonlaice/quinhao

WHAT IT DOES

Mozambican law directs 2.75% of the mining and petroleum production tax to the communities
that host the extraction. The money is transferred and reported as fully executed. What is
missing is anything a resident can act on: why their locality received that particular
amount, and whether anything was built with it.

Quinhao turns six years of those transfers into a page a person in Nyamanhumbir or Benga
can read, compare and question. It covers 56 localities across all 10 provinces, in English,
Portuguese and French, with a metical/dollar toggle. The whole page is one 80 KB file with
no external requests, no fonts and no frameworks, so it loads on a weak connection and keeps
working offline once opened.

INFORMATION SOURCES

Everything comes from documents published by EITI Mozambique at eiti.org - five source PDFs
covering six fiscal years:
- 2019: 2020 EITI Report, Table 42 (locality level)
- 2021: 2021 EITI Report, Table 46 (locality level)
- 2022: 2022 EITI Report, Table 45 (individual project level)
- 2023 and 2024: 2023-2024 EITI Report, Annex 6.1 (locality + commodity) and Annex 6.2
  (transfers to provincial directorates)

Two supporting sources: administrative boundaries from geoBoundaries (ADM1 and ADM2, gbOpen)
and official annual average exchange rates from the World Bank (indicator PA.NUS.FCRF).

APPROACH TO TRUST AND ACCURACY

The subject is the credibility of public numbers, so the tool cannot ask to be trusted. It
has to be checkable.

Nothing is typed by hand. A script downloads each source PDF and parses it; the CSVs in the
repository are build outputs, not manual transcription.

The build fails rather than publishes a wrong number. After parsing, the script asserts each
year's computed total against the total printed in the source: 88.0, 73.4, 44,608,666.89,
77.1 and 318.7 million meticais, plus 840.30 for the provincial annex. If a parser drifts,
the run stops. The same gate applies to the map: a district name that does not match a
polygon fails the build instead of silently disappearing. That check caught two real errors,
"Alto Moloucue" against "Alto Molocue", and Pemba filed as "Cidade De Pemba".

Manual corrections are explicit and auditable. The source PDFs merge district cells
vertically and centre text inside them, so column position cannot always distinguish a
(district, locality) pair from a (locality, activity) one. The affected rows are listed in a
CORRECTIONS table in the code with the reason stated, rather than hidden inside a heuristic.

Currency is converted at each year's own official rate, never at today's rate. The metical
moved from 62.55 to 69.47 to 63.91 per dollar over this period; converting 2019 figures at a
2024 rate would misstate them by about a tenth.

What the data cannot show is stated on the page, not buried. Execution is reported at around
100%, which means the money left the treasury, not that anything was built. The 2022
project-level file ships marked as a draft because its labels are unreliable. And the
strongest finding is EITI's own sentence, quoted directly on the page: the budget execution
report does not allow anyone to determine what criterion split the money between communities,
nor what activities the funds went to.

WHAT THE DATA SHOWS

In 2024, affected communities received 318.7 million meticais while provincial directorates
received 840.3 million - the communities living on top of the ruby, the coal and the gas get
less than a third of what goes to the provincial capitals. The 2020 report also shows that
2.75% of the 2018 production tax should have been 100.59 million meticais while 87.8 million
was allocated, a 12.79 million gap the Ministry explains by counting only the mining tax and
not the petroleum one. The money is heavily concentrated: 86.7 of Tete's 98.0 million lands
in a single district, Moatize.

HOW AI CODING TOOLS WERE USED

Claude Code was used throughout, and the most valuable work was not writing code.

It killed the original idea. The first concept was "allocated versus actually transferred".
Reading the source annexes showed execution reported at 100% across the board - that story
did not exist. The finding that replaced it came out of the documents, not out of a prompt.

PDF archaeology: locating the community-transfer tables across five reports in three
different layouts, including project-level spending that had not been extracted before.

Parser development against a hard check: each per-year parser was written, run and corrected
until all five published totals matched. Several bugs surfaced only because an assertion
failed.

Verification rather than assumption: data availability was checked for both candidate ideas
before committing. The national public procurement portal turned out to be returning a
database error, which is why this project uses EITI data instead. A photograph offered for
the page was rejected because it showed an offshore oil platform of a type Mozambique does
not have. The chart palette was run through a colourblind-safety validator rather than chosen
by eye, and the live page was driven in a browser at phone width in all three languages.

LIMITS AND NEXT STEPS

The 2022 project-level file needs a manual pass over its 41 rows. There is no field
verification layer yet - that is the natural next step, and the reason the page ends by
telling a resident who is answerable (the District Secretariat and the Locality Consultative
Council), what the law permits the money to be spent on, and three specific questions they
have the right to ask. Collecting those answers would turn 56 localities into a monitoring
network.

EITI reporting is standardised across more than 50 implementing countries, so the parsing
approach, the verification gate and the page port directly to any of them that publishes
subnational transfers. Running cost is zero: a static page and a script that re-runs when the
next report is published.
